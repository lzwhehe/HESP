\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Setting and interface}
An episode begins with an alert and a finite set of hypotheses $\mathcal{H}$ that contains the candidate explanations plus an explicit \textit{other}. The agent may call read-only probes $a \in \mathcal{A}$ that cost $c(a)$ each, within budgets on tool calls, total cost, planner decisions, and wall time. At each step the planner receives a structured request: the task, the probe catalogue with descriptions and costs, the observation history, feedback on blocked proposals, the remaining budgets and, depending on the configuration, the ledger. It returns one JSON decision:
\texttt{action} (propose a probe), \texttt{finish} (a hypothesis plus the observation IDs it cites), or \texttt{stop} (abstain). \Cref{fig:overview} shows how the controller mediates each decision.

\subsection{Hypothesis ledger}
The ledger holds a posterior $p(h)$ over $\mathcal{H}$. Each probe comes with a predictive table $P(o \mid h, a)$ over its outcome vocabulary. After observing $o$, the ledger applies Bayes' rule and records, for every hypothesis, whether the observation raised, lowered, or left unchanged its score (\textit{support}, \textit{against}, \textit{neutral}). A transient failure, such as an HTTP 503, is logged but is not evidence, and the probe may be retried. An outcome that is impossible under every hypothesis marks a table conflict and is skipped. The environment exposes a \emph{state version}. When the incident changes under the agent (the \textit{drift} variant), the version advances and current scores are reset to the prior. Earlier evidence stays in the record but no longer counts as current.

\subsection{Probe selection}
For every legal probe (untried in the current state, affordable, prerequisites met), the controller computes the expected information gain
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{EIG}(a) &= H(p) - \textstyle\sum_{o} P(o \mid a)\, H\big(p(\cdot \mid o, a)\big),\\
P(o \mid a) &= \textstyle\sum_h p(h)\, P(o \mid h, a),
\end{align*}
where $H$ is Shannon entropy and $p(\cdot \mid o, a)$ is the posterior after observing $o$. It ranks the probes by $\mathrm{EIG}(a)/c(a)$. In the \hesp{} configurations the controller executes its own choice whatever the planner proposed. Configurations differ in whether the planner is shown the ranking. The \emph{blind} configurations show neither the ranking nor the selector name, so the planner's prompt is identical whichever selector runs, which a unit test checks byte for byte. A \emph{random} selector, which picks uniformly among legal probes, serves as the control.

\subsection{Finishing, verification, and audit}
\paragraph{State-guarded finish.} A planner's \texttt{finish} is accepted only if at least one cited observation is current-state evidence that supports the claimed hypothesis and the hypothesis has current score at least $\tau = 0.8$. A rejected finish is returned to the planner as feedback.

\paragraph{Controller-side stop.} The controller may also conclude on its own. Before each planner call, and when a budget runs out, it applies the same acceptance rule to the current leader. If the rule is met, it cites up to three supporting current-state observations and ends the episode. The planner may still finish or abstain earlier, and nothing in its prompt reveals the rule.

\paragraph{Independent verifier.} A verdict counts as \emph{verified} only if the hypothesis equals the cause in effect and at least one cited current-state observation carries a signature that this cause alone produces. A verdict reached purely by elimination, or backed only by a generic ``malicious'' threat-intelligence hit that several attacks share, does not verify. The verifier is not reachable through the planner interface.

\paragraph{Journal and audit.} Every prediction is written to an append-only journal before the probe runs. After each episode, an audit checks event ordering, observation provenance, counters, budgets, citations, and that every controller-made verdict met its rule. All reported episodes pass the audit.

\subsection{Design alternatives}
\label{sec:alternatives}
Each choice above replaced an alternative we implemented and measured.

\paragraph{Ranking shown to the planner, planner decides.} The controller could publish its ranking and let the planner pick. With the ledger visible, small models still pick poorly (\cref{sec:threat}), and showing the ranking changes completion by at most $+0.06$ once the controller selects (\cref{sec:rq-rank}). The controller therefore executes its own choice.

\paragraph{Non-myopic lookahead.} A budget-aware two-step lookahead selector beat EIG/cost by $+0.17$ to $+0.21$ on the task family we developed it on. On a held-out family of an earlier study (3{,}888 episodes) it matched or trailed EIG/cost, by $-0.125$ for the 7B planner, and on sec-triage it completed 0.889, 0.972, and 0.958 against 0.958, 1.000, and 1.000 for the three Qwen models. Its gains came from fitting the development family, so we kept the greedy rule.

\paragraph{Likelihoods from the model.} Asking the planner for $P(o \mid h, a)$ removes the need for development data but leaves completion at 0.069 to 0.375 (\cref{sec:rq-tables}). Counting outcomes in LLM-free development runs works with as few as five episodes per case in our environment.

\paragraph{Stopping in the prompt.} Showing the planner a posterior above 0.999 and telling it that no legal probe is left does not make Llama-3.1-8B conclude. A stopping rule enforced by the controller does. Its cost is that the controller concludes by elimination when the evidence allows, which our verifier does not accept (\cref{sec:rq-stop}).

\paragraph{No finish guard.} Without the guard, a planner can cite any observation. The guard changes completion by at most $+0.04$, but it guarantees that every accepted verdict cites current supporting evidence, so we keep it for that property.
