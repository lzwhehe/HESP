\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Limitations} This is a controlled mechanism study. All task families are simulated. Our raw-log studies render logs from templates of our own, with one drifted format, one claim wording, and one log-line forgery, so they measure the reading channel for these formats and this attacker; real telemetry is noisier and larger, and an attacker can phrase claims we did not test. The reader-trust rule is evaluated with a rule parser that is immune to the tested attacks by construction (it reads the first record of each probe); a rule parser that could be fooled would move that trust. The second family removes our own choice of causes, but its outcomes are an LLM's annotation rather than observed telemetry, and replacing one indistinguishable rule conditions it on causes the catalogue can tell apart. The families are small (24 tasks from eight causes, 12 rules, and three comp-triage scenarios), the first two nearly deterministic, development and evaluation episodes share a generator, and our intervals generalize at most to new episodes of these tasks, not to new causes. The candidate causes are given and mutually exclusive, \textit{other} is a residual with no development cases, and the environment announces when the incident changes; new causes, concurrent causes, and undetected change are outside the study. In drifting tasks the switch happens after the second probe, so an agent's pace decides whether it meets the drift. Our verifier requires one observation that only the claimed cause produces; correct verdicts reached by combination or elimination count as failures, and the comp-triage scenarios that test combinations are few and written by us (\S\ref{sec:policy}). The prompt comparison selected among four variants, on two small models and one family; the reader attacks ran on the same two models. The text-channel tests use four injection texts and two forgeries in one environment, and an attacker who forges two independent source groups would defeat the corroboration rule too. The v1.1 to v1.3 LLM runs used two different GPUs, and the small models a different serving stack. Public data could not provide a confirmation on real telemetry (\S\ref{sec:realdata}). Finally, we made errors of our own, from a misreported interval and lost journals to reading ExCyTIn test questions during a feasibility check; Appendix~\ref{app:errata} documents each of them. One, the table asymmetry of E-7, lay inside a primary comparison of the table-source study; later studies that removed it support the same conclusion, which does not make the original comparison clean.

\paragraph{Security Implications} The three channels differ in what contains them. Text that reaches the planner is contained by the finish guard, because the guard accepts only evidence that is in the ledger, and text never enters it; the injection still costs analyst time, since rejected dismissals end in abstentions and escalations. Evidence that reaches the ledger is not contained by the guard: the controller trusts its probes, and acting on the most informative observation first makes a single forged source decisive. Corroboration across source groups contains it, as long as the attacker forges one source. Readings produced by a model are not contained by corroboration at all, because the attacker's text travels with every probe's response, so every ``source'' the ledger counts can carry the same forged reading; under a reader that adopts every claim, the attack closes every actionable case as the authorized scan with two probes. What contains it is a boundary between readers: the rule that a benign verdict must rest on a trusted reading. Four design rules follow. The guard should always be on, because an unguarded small model overrides the ledger (\S\ref{sec:external}, \S\ref{sec:injection}). A benign verdict should require corroboration from distinct source groups, since two probes can read one poisoned feed; which sources are independent is a property of the deployment. A model should read only what a fixed parser cannot, and what it reads should never close a case: the parser, not the planner, is the component to harden. And a benign verdict should remain a recommendation that an analyst confirms, so that the residual attack is a delayed response rather than an unattended closure.

\paragraph{What the Model Is For} With structured observations, the controller accounts for all of the measured gain. The earlier advantage of strong models that decided when to stop came from waiting for a confirming signature, and a confirmation rule written into the controller reproduces it without an LLM (\S\ref{sec:cost}). Our pre-registered test found no incremental value of Qwen2.5-72B over a controller with an explicit confirmation rule, no other model showed one, and a prompt selected to help the baselines did not close the gap. Within discrete, structured observations, the contribution of this work is the allocation of control and its attack surface, not a system that needs an LLM. The role that remains is reading (\S\ref{sec:rawlog}): when the log format changes, a rule parser reads nothing and every case escalates, whereas small models read the new format almost without error. That role is where the model is needed and where it is dangerous, and the reader-trust rule lets a deployment have the reading without the closure: under a drifted format an attack can still be confirmed from model-parsed readings, while every benign case is escalated until a trusted reading exists.

\paragraph{Deployment} We recommend \hesp{} with EIG/cost selection, the finish guard, the confirmation stop, source-group corroboration for benign verdicts, the recovery rule after rejected finishes, and, wherever the model reads, the reader-trust rule with a rule parser first; verdicts are handed to an analyst together with their cited evidence and journal. This configuration sits at the safe end of the frontier in \cref{tab:v12policy}: where causes share tools and no single observation separates them, it escalates most cases rather than risk closing an attack as the legitimate activity it resembles; a deployment that values automation more can accept joint evidence and should expect such mistakes. The prediction tables are the main deployment cost. Ours ran every probe on cases of known cause; resolved tickets record only the probes an analyst chose, so tables estimated from them face selection bias and missing counterfactual outcomes. The confirmation rule adds one more requirement: at least one probe must single out each cause. When the tables blur two causes it escalates every case, so it keeps errors low by giving up automation, and the ten-fold specificity threshold sets that trade-off (\S\ref{sec:policy}).
