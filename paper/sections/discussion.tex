\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the scores still tell us} Our results do not say that the audited benchmarks are useless. On ExCyTIn, the ranking of strong agents is the same on questions without any shortcut, and the agents do not take the shortcut that exists. What the results do say is narrower and, for a defender, more practical. First, an absolute score has to be read against the floor that a fixed rule reaches: below it, a score is no evidence of investigation. Second, a high score on a benchmark whose difficulty tracks the required investigation only weakly says little about the cases a SOC cares most about, those in which nothing names the answer and the malicious activity looks routine. Third, on SIABench, GUIDE, and OTRF the shortcuts are in the data, so no model behavior can repair them; only new data can.

\paragraph{Why agents ignore the shortcut} The ExCyTIn agents receive a fixed prompt that tells them to explore a database of more than 20 tables, and they follow it. A different prompt, or an agent trained on the benchmark, could learn to look up the named alert first and would then gain up to the floor at no cost. Benchmarks that are also meant for training, as ExCyTIn's question generator is~\citep{excytin}, are exposed to exactly this, which is a reason to remove the cue rather than rely on agents not finding it.

\paragraph{Limitations} \emph{Coverage.} We audited four public benchmarks and our own environments; recent benchmarks such as AuditBench, CyberClear, DiagChain, and SIR-Bench~\citep{auditbench,cyberclear,diagchain,sirbench} were not audited, and SIR-Bench in particular is built to separate investigation from repeating the alert. \emph{Scoring.} Our lookups are scored by exact match and the agents by an LLM judge with partial credit, so the floors are conservative; we did not re-run the agents. \emph{Post hoc rules.} The SIABench rule was found after looking at the data; it rests on a documented property of CIC-IDS2017, and on 35 alerts any rule is fragile. The GUIDE and OTRF analyses use the training split and 27 recordings, respectively. \emph{Release mismatch.} We wrote and froze the ExCyTIn rules on the latest release and found later that the published scores use an earlier one; we registered the analysis of the earlier release before running it, and both releases give the same picture. \emph{Exploratory analyses.} The trace statistics (three runs) and the split by path length were not registered. \emph{Our environments.} The constructed environments are our own and serve to show that the problem is not specific to other authors; they are not evidence about real SOC data.
