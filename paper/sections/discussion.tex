\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Limitations} This is a controlled mechanism study. All task families are simulated. Our raw-log study renders logs from templates of our own, with one drifted format and one injection field, so it measures the reading step only for these formats; real telemetry is noisier and larger. The second family removes our own choice of causes, but its outcomes are an LLM's annotation rather than observed telemetry, and replacing one indistinguishable rule conditions it on causes the catalogue can tell apart. Both families are small (24 tasks from eight causes, and 12 rules) and nearly deterministic, development and evaluation episodes share a generator, and our intervals generalize at most to new episodes of these tasks, not to new causes. The candidate causes are given and mutually exclusive, \textit{other} is a residual with no development cases, and the environment announces when the incident changes; new causes, concurrent causes, and undetected change are outside the study. In drifting tasks the switch happens after the second probe, so an agent's pace decides whether it meets the drift. Our verifier requires one observation that only the claimed cause produces; correct verdicts reached by combination or elimination count as failures, and the comp-triage scenarios that test combinations are few and written by us (\S\ref{sec:fair}). Our baselines used the same prompt as \hesp{}, not a separately developed one. The adversarial tests use four injection texts and two forgeries in one environment, and an attacker who forges two independent source groups would defeat the corroboration rule too. The v1.1 LLM runs used two different GPUs, and the small models a different serving stack. Public data could not provide a confirmation on real telemetry (\S\ref{sec:realdata}). Finally, we made errors of our own, from a misreported interval and lost journals to reading ExCyTIn test questions during a feasibility check; Appendix~\ref{app:errata} documents each of them. One, the table asymmetry of E-7, lay inside a primary comparison of the table-source study; later studies that removed it support the same conclusion, which does not make the original comparison clean.

\paragraph{Security Implications} The results separate two kinds of attacker influence. Text that reaches the model is contained by the finish guard: an instruction in a log field changed no guarded verdict, whereas every model that decided and reached its own verdict followed it, and removing the raw text alone also blocked the dismissal, at no cost for the 32B and 72B models but leaving most of the 7B model's cases unresolved. The injection still costs analyst time, because rejected dismissals end in abstentions and escalations. Evidence that reaches the ledger is not contained: the controller trusts its probes, and acting on the most informative observation first makes a single forged source decisive. Three design rules follow. The guard should always be on, because an unguarded small model overrides the ledger (\S\ref{sec:external}, \S\ref{sec:adversarial}). A benign verdict should require corroboration from distinct source groups, since two probes can read one poisoned feed; which sources are independent is a property of the deployment. And a benign verdict should remain a recommendation that an analyst confirms, so that the residual attack is a delayed response rather than an unattended closure.

\paragraph{Where the Model Is Still Needed} With structured observations, the controller accounts for all of the measured gain. The earlier advantage of strong models that decided when to stop came from waiting for a confirming signature, and a confirmation rule written into the controller reproduces it without an LLM (\S\ref{sec:fair}). Our pre-registered test found no incremental value of Qwen2.5-72B over a controller with an explicit confirmation rule, and no other model showed one; within discrete, structured observations, the contribution of this work is the allocation of control and its security boundary, not a system that needs an LLM. The role that remains is reading (\S\ref{sec:rawlog}): when the log format changes, a rule parser reads nothing and every case escalates, whereas small models read the new format almost without error. The same reading reopens the attack surface, since a model parser can adopt an attacker's claimed reading; in our runs the ledger's cross-checking kept those adoptions from closing an attack, at a cost in escalations. A deployment should therefore let the model read but not decide, validate parsed labels against a second source where possible, and treat the parser, not the planner, as the component to harden.

\paragraph{Deployment} We recommend \hesp{} with EIG/cost selection, the finish guard, the confirmation stop, source-group corroboration for benign verdicts, and the recovery rule after rejected finishes, with verdicts handed to an analyst together with their cited evidence and journal. This configuration sits at the safe end of the frontier in \cref{tab:v12policy}: where causes share tools and no single observation separates them, it escalates most cases rather than risk closing an attack as the legitimate activity it resembles; a deployment that values automation more can accept joint evidence and should expect such mistakes. The prediction tables are the main deployment cost. Ours ran every probe on cases of known cause; resolved tickets record only the probes an analyst chose, so tables estimated from them face selection bias and missing counterfactual outcomes. The confirmation rule adds one more requirement: at least one probe must single out each cause. When the tables blur two causes it escalates every case, so it keeps errors low by giving up automation, and the ten-fold specificity threshold sets that trade-off (\S\ref{sec:mismatch}).
