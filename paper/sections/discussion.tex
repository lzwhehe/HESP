\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the model is still needed.} Our results locate the value of a local model precisely. When observations arrive as discrete outcomes and the prediction tables come from development data, the procedure does most of the work. The controller alone completes 0.917 of cases, and small models inside the controller do no better. Two roles remain for the model. The first is judging when evidence suffices: strong models that decide when to stop reach 1.000 because they wait for a confirming signature. The same judgment could be written into the controller as a rule, which we leave for future work. The second is producing the discrete observations in the first place. A real probe returns log lines, not the label \texttt{high\_entropy}, and turning one into the other requires reading. Our environment performs that translation itself, so our results do not measure it. The OTRF case in \cref{sec:case} shows what it involves: recognizing that a task's creator is a person rather than a machine account, or that a logon came over the network.

\paragraph{Different small models lack different things.} For Llama-3.1-8B, the missing piece was the stopping rule. For Qwen2.5-7B, it was both the stopping rule and probe selection. For 32B and 72B models, taking probe choice away was harmful. A deployment therefore cannot treat ``let the controller choose'' as free. An evaluation that bundles what to probe with when to stop cannot tell which failure a model has.

\paragraph{Knowledge versus procedure.} The small models cannot supply the probe-outcome likelihoods themselves: elicited tables leave Qwen2.5-7B at 0.069. The controller's tables carry that knowledge, and they were counted from development episodes of the same generator. In a real deployment they would have to come from resolved tickets, an expert, or an LLM prior corrected by data. Which of these is accurate enough is an open question.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One hand-built environment.} All confirmatory evidence comes from one task family that we designed to defeat shortcuts. It is small (24 tasks) and nearly deterministic, and development and evaluation episodes share a generator.
  \item \textbf{A given hypothesis set.} The candidate causes are supplied, and \textit{other} is only a residual. Generating new hypotheses is outside this work.
  \item \textbf{A strict verifier.} A verdict must cite an observation carrying the cause's own signature, so a correct verdict reached by elimination counts as a failure. This rule applies to every configuration since the initial study and makes our completion rates conservative. It is also the whole cost of a controller-side stop.
  \item \textbf{No adversarial evaluation.} We do not test evidence manipulation or prompt injection through log content (\cref{sec:threat}).
  \item \textbf{Models and hardware.} Two model families, five sizes, one temperature, and one serving stack.
  \item \textbf{No real-data confirmation.} \Cref{sec:realdata} explains why public data could not provide it. The case study is qualitative.
  \item \textbf{Our own errors.} We misreported one interval (E-1), lost the journals of one study (E-2), found a ranking leak into a control arm (E-4) and a table asymmetry that favoured the baseline (E-7), and corrected the ground truth of a descriptive metric twice (E-6). \Cref{app:errata} documents all of them. None affects a confirmed primary endpoint.
\end{itemize}
