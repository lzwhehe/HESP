\begin{abstract}
LLM agents are being trusted with alert triage and investigation in security operations, largely on the strength of benchmark scores. A benchmark measures investigation only if its items cannot be answered without investigating. We audit four public benchmarks and datasets for such agents (ExCyTIn-Bench, SIABench, GUIDE, and OTRF) and three environments of our own, using fixed rules that see only what the agent sees, contain no LLM, and skip the intended investigation. Every benchmark admits such a rule. On ExCyTIn-Bench, most questions name the alert that holds the answer, and looking it up beats \oBelowGraph{} of \oModels{} reported models; on SIABench, one IP address separates all \siaN{} alerts; on GUIDE, an organization's past grade for the same detector is 91\% accurate. Published per-question logs of \lgModels{} ExCyTIn runs then show that the agents do not use the shortcut: success is no higher when the question names the alert, and the ranking holds on shortcut-free questions. The deeper problem is that success depends only weakly on the investigation a question requires; the best agent fails 31\% of the questions one lookup answers. We give checks for benchmark builders, show that pairing each attack with a benign twin removes the single-observation shortcut, and release per-question shortcut flags.
\end{abstract}
