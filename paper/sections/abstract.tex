\begin{abstract}
Organizations that cannot send telemetry to hosted models must automate alert triage with small open-weight LLMs, and these models fail at the investigation procedure: they probe without converging, do not commit to a verdict, or follow instructions written into the logs. We ask which triage decisions should be taken from the model, and what security boundary results. \hesp{} is a controller that keeps a ledger of candidate causes, selects read-only probes by expected information gain per cost, accepts only verdicts backed by current evidence, and can end an investigation itself. In a controlled study on three synthetic task families (seven studies with internally frozen protocols, five models from 7B to 72B, 21{,}278 audited LLM episodes), choosing what to probe and deciding when to stop are separate failures. With the verifier's evidence standard, the controller completes every case without an LLM; a pre-registered comparison finds no gain from letting Qwen2.5-72B decide when to stop, and baselines given an equally clear prompt still fall far short. Requiring one decisive observation trades automation for safety: joint evidence resolves more cases in which causes share tools, but closes some attacks as the legitimate activity they resemble. The finish guard blocks injected instructions to dismiss an alert on every model; a forged source defeats the controller unless benign verdicts need corroboration from distinct sources. The model's remaining role is reading raw logs, where small models handle a changed format that defeats a rule parser but can adopt an attacker's claimed reading.
\end{abstract}
