\begin{abstract}
Organizations that keep security telemetry on their premises triage alerts with small open-weight LLMs, and part of the evidence such an agent reads is written by the attacker. We ask where the attacker gets in, and show that the answer depends on which triage decisions the model is allowed to make. \hesp{} is a controller that keeps a ledger of candidate causes, selects read-only probes by expected information gain per cost, accepts only verdicts backed by current evidence, and can end an investigation itself; the model proposes, and, when logs are raw text, reads. In a controlled study on three synthetic task families (eight prospectively frozen studies, five models from 7B to 72B, 23{,}582 audited LLM episodes) we find three channels, one per allocation of control. When the model decides, every model that reaches its own verdict follows an instruction planted in a log field to dismiss the alert; a finish guard that accepts only ledger-backed verdicts blocks it on every model. When the controller decides, its trust in the most informative probe makes one forged source decisive; requiring corroboration from distinct source groups for benign verdicts restores safety at a measured cost. When the model must read raw logs, the one role the controller cannot take over, an attacker who writes a consistent benign story into every log's free-text field defeats source-group corroboration, because the forged reading arrives with every source; a reader-trust rule, under which model-parsed observations may confirm an attack but never a benign verdict, closes the channel, and honest benign cases pay for it whenever the rule parser cannot read. Taking the decisions from the model costs nothing we could measure: with the verifier's evidence standard the controller alone completes every case in the two deterministic families, no model adds to it, and clearer prompts do not rescue the baselines. The evidence standard is itself a trade-off: joint evidence resolves more cases in which causes share tools but closes some attacks as the legitimate activity they resemble.
\end{abstract}
