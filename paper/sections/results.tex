\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Does the controller help small models?}
\label{sec:rq-help}
The initial study (1{,}944 episodes, designer tables) compared full \hesp{} (EIG/cost selection with the finish guard) against Memory-only, in which the planner sees the same ledger but picks its own probes. Verified completion rose by $+0.889$ $[+0.75,+1.00]$ for Qwen2.5-7B and by $+0.250$ $[+0.10,+0.42]$ for 32B. For 72B the gain was $+0.111$, with an interval that touches zero ($[0.000,+0.236]$). We expected the gain to shrink with model size, and it did. Two defects of this study shaped the next ones. Its tables came from the environment's generator, and its random-selection control could still see the ranking. Its per-episode journals were also not archived (errata E-1 to E-5).
\takeaway{The controller helps small models most, but this first study could not rule out that the gain came from oracle tables.}

\subsection{Must the prediction tables come from an oracle?}
\label{sec:rq-tables}
The table-source study replaced the generator-derived tables with tables counted from LLM-free development episodes. If the gain came from oracle knowledge, it should vanish with counted tables. Its single primary endpoint was the 7B comparison against Memory-only.

  \caption{Table-source study: verified completion by the source of the prediction tables (72 episodes per cell). The 7B comparison against Memory-only is the pre-registered primary endpoint; 32B and 72B are exploratory. Intervals are 95\% task-cluster bootstrap.}

\paragraph{Counted tables match the oracle.} With tables counted from $k{=}20$ development episodes per cause and variant, \hesp{} lifts Qwen2.5-7B from 0.125 to 1.000 ($+0.875$ $[+0.74,+0.97]$; 22 tasks better, none worse; \cref{tab:v06}). Completion equals that of the designer tables on all three models. It saturates at $k{=}5$, and $k{=}1$ loses at most one episode per model. This saturation reflects how nearly deterministic the environment is, not a general data-efficiency property.

\paragraph{The price of not being an oracle is one row.} Counted tables cost 1.2 to 2.4 more probe units per episode than designer tables. An LLM-free check locates the entire gap in the row for \textit{other}, the one hypothesis development data never exhibits. Substituting the designer's row for \textit{other} alone restores completion and cost exactly, from 0.875 at 3.53 units to 1.000 at 2.54 in the scripted setting.

\paragraph{Models cannot write the tables themselves.} With tables elicited from the planner, completion drops to 0.069, 0.375, and 0.375. Under the finish guard, the 32B and 72B models complete no case caused by credential stuffing, SQL injection, DNS command-and-control, the exposed admin panel, or the vulnerable component, and every case of the other three causes.
\takeaway{Tables counted from a few LLM-free development runs are as good as the oracle, and the model cannot write them itself.}

\subsection{Is it the ranking, or taking the choice away?}
\label{sec:rq-rank}
The decomposition study removes the ranking leak with blind variants, in which the planner receives the same prompt under every selector, and adds the Llama~3.1 family. If the ranking itself matters, EIG/cost should beat random selection even when the planner's prompt is identical. Its primary endpoints were the ranking effect on Qwen2.5-7B (blind EIG/cost against blind random) and full \hesp{} against Memory-only on Llama-3.1-8B. \Cref{fig:decomposition} shows the two components of the gain; \cref{tab:app-v08,tab:app-v08terms} in the appendix give all values.

The hollow marker (Qwen 7B, ranking) is a primary endpoint with a 97.5\% interval; the others are exploratory 95\% intervals. Amber marks intervals entirely below zero. Llama-3.1-8B never concludes, so every contrast on it is zero.}
  \label{fig:decomposition}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{The ranking itself helps.} With identical planner information, EIG/cost selection beats random selection by $+0.306$ $[+0.14,+0.47]$ on Qwen2.5-7B, confirming the first primary endpoint. The effect is similar on every model that concludes: $+0.35$, $+0.35$, and $+0.26$ on Qwen 32B, Qwen 72B, and Llama 70B, all with intervals excluding zero.

\paragraph{Taking the choice away helps weak models and hurts strong ones.} Replacing the model's own probe choices with random ones raises completion by $+0.556$ for the 7B model, whose own choices are worse than random: it keeps probing until the budget runs out and rarely concludes. The same replacement lowers completion by $0.222$ and $0.278$ for Qwen 32B and 72B, whose choices beat random. For a small model, the controller therefore contributes discipline and ranking. For a large model it contributes ranking alone, which outweighs the lost choices only because the ranking is informative. Showing the ranking to the planner and adding the finish guard each change completion by at most $+0.06$.

\paragraph{Selecting probes does not make a model conclude.} The second primary endpoint failed. Llama-3.1-8B completes none of its 360 episodes in any configuration. It makes 3{,}389 decisions, all requests for another probe, even when the ledger posterior exceeds 0.999 and the controller reports that no legal probe is left. Its outputs are valid JSON throughout. The controller decided what to probe, but the planner still decided when to stop.
\takeaway{The ranking helps every model that concludes; taking the choice away helps only weak planners; and selecting probes cannot rescue a model that never stops.}

\subsection{What to probe versus when to stop}
\label{sec:rq-stop}
The stopping study crosses who selects probes with who may stop, adds a random-selection control, and puts both primary endpoints on Llama-3.1-8B: whether a controller stop rescues it, and whether controller selection still helps once the stop is supplied. Before the run, an LLM-free simulation predicted about 0.9 for the first endpoint if the model kept never concluding, so we treated it as nearly mechanical and the second as the informative test. \Cref{fig:probe-stop} and \cref{tab:v09} show the results.

\paragraph{A controller stop rescues the model that never concludes.} With the controller allowed to stop, Llama-3.1-8B rises from 0 to 0.917 ($+0.917$ $[+0.79,+1.00]$, 22 tasks better, none worse), as predicted.

\paragraph{For that model, the stop accounts for the gain.} Once the stop is supplied, Llama-3.1-8B's own probe choices reach 0.861 and controller selection 0.917, a difference of $+0.056$ $[-0.07,+0.18]$. The interval admits a moderate benefit, so we conclude only that selection adds no detectable completion for this model. It does halve probe cost (2.35 against 4.78 units). Its own choices perform like the fixed catalogue order, which reaches 0.847 at 4.85 units in the LLM-free run.

\paragraph{Small models fail differently.} Qwen2.5-7B tells a different story. A controller stop alone lifts it from 0.083 to 0.694, and controller selection then adds $+0.222$ $[+0.08,+0.39]$ (\cref{tab:app-v09terms}). Its failures in the decomposition study were therefore partly a refusal to conclude and partly poor probing, whereas Llama-3.1-8B's were almost entirely a refusal to conclude. A controller cannot know in advance which part a given model lacks, so it has to supply both.

\paragraph{The ranking effect replicates on all five models.} Under a controller stop, EIG/cost beats random selection by $+0.17$ to $+0.21$ on every model, including Llama-3.1-8B, all with intervals excluding zero.

\paragraph{The controller concludes by elimination; strong models wait for confirmation.} On models that already conclude, allowing the controller to stop lowers completion by 0.01 to 0.08. Every failure of the controller-stop arm, six episodes per model, is a correct DNS command-and-control verdict that the controller reached by eliminating the alternatives. The verifier requires a confirming signature and rejects it, as the protocol anticipated before the run. When Qwen 32B and Llama 70B decide when to stop, they keep probing until the DNS evidence appears and complete every case. The only other failures are three drift episodes in which Qwen 72B finished first with the wrong cause.

\paragraph{The controller alone comes close.} The LLM-free run completes 0.917 of cases at 2.35 units, the lowest cost of any configuration. When the controller both selects and stops, four of the five models also score exactly 0.917 at the same cost, so the model contributes almost nothing there. The configurations that beat it are those in which a strong model decides when the evidence suffices.
\takeaway{What to probe and when to stop are separate failures. A controller stop fixes the second, the ranking fixes the first, and different small models need different parts.}

\subsection{Security-facing behaviour}
\label{sec:security}
\Cref{tab:security} breaks the table-source study's verdicts down by error type. With counted tables, \hesp{} issued a verdict in all 216 episodes and none named the wrong cause. The baselines fail in scale-dependent ways. The 7B baselines rarely conclude, leaving 88 to 97\% of episodes unresolved. The larger ReAct-style baselines conclude but are often wrong: they call 26\% (32B) and 37\% (72B) of actionable incidents benign and cite invalid evidence in 22 to 31\% of citations. Giving the model the ledger (Memory-only) removes most of these errors. In the stopping study, the configuration with controller selection and controller stop never called an actionable incident benign on any of the five models. These metrics are descriptive and were not pre-registered as endpoints.
\takeaway{With the finish guard, no verdict issued through the controller named the wrong cause in the table-source study's 216 episodes; unguided large models most often err by calling real attacks benign.}
