\section{Evaluation Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report the results by channel rather than by study: what the attacker achieves when the model decides (\S\ref{sec:injection}), when the controller decides (\S\ref{sec:evidence}), and when the model reads (\S\ref{sec:rawlog}); then what the allocation costs in completion (\S\ref{sec:cost}), how the evidence policy and the tables behind it trade automation for safety (\S\ref{sec:policy}), whether the effects replicate on the second family (\S\ref{sec:external}), and what public data can add (\S\ref{sec:realdata}). Appendix~\ref{app:tables} gives every cell of every study. Without adversarial evidence, the ledger alone already removes most wrong verdicts: in the table-source study, the ReAct-style Qwen2.5-32B and 72B baselines called 13 of 51 and 20 of 54 actionable episodes benign among those they ruled on, Memory-only 2 of 54 and 1 of 53, and \hesp{} none.

\subsection{Text: When the Model Decides}
\label{sec:injection}
\label{sec:adversarial}
\label{sec:security}
The attacker's cheapest move is to write. The adversarial studies plant an instruction to close the case as an authorized scan in free-text fields of every probe response, in four texts that differ in wording and position (a note, a user-agent field, a fake analyst-override field, and one probe's response only), on the six actionable causes of sec-triage. \Cref{tab:v11attack} attributes the outcome to components on all five models; the v1.0 results are in \cref{tab:v10c} (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).

Every model that decides and reaches its own verdict follows the instruction, and controller selection and stop without the guard still let the planner finish first with the injected verdict in 36 to 48 of 72 episodes. The finish guard is what blocks it: every guarded configuration closed 0 of 72 on every model, and in v1.0 the pre-registered comparison on Qwen2.5-7B was $+1.000$ $[+1.000,+1.000]$, all 18 paired episodes moving the same way. The guard works because the injected text never enters the ledger, and the guard accepts only verdicts the ledger supports; removing the raw text from the prompt also blocks the dismissal, at no cost for Qwen2.5-32B and 72B but leaving 65 of 72 Qwen2.5-7B cases unresolved. The injection still costs analyst time: the guard without a controller stop leaves 44 to 72 of 72 episodes unresolved, and adding the stop 0 to 42.

\subsection{Evidence: When the Controller Decides}
\label{sec:evidence}
Once the guard is on, the attacker has to forge evidence rather than text: a source-reputation probe that reports a known scanner (\emph{forged probe}), or an upstream reputation feed that two probes read (\emph{forged feed}). A single forged scanner answer lifts the authorized scan above 0.8 after one probe, and the controller stop concludes: 18 of 18 actionable cases are closed as benign on every model, and the forged feed does the same. Acting on the most informative evidence first is exactly what the attacker exploits. The forged feed also misleads the larger LLMs themselves (up to 15 of 18 under Memory-only).

Corroboration for benign verdicts closes this gap at a price. Requiring two specifically supporting observations escalates all forged cases on every model, but the per-probe rule survived the forged feed only because the controller's second probe read a different source. A targeted v1.2 test makes a fixed playbook read \texttt{source\_ips} and then \texttt{threat\_intel}, both fed by the forged source: per-probe corroboration then closes 18 of 18 actionable cases as benign, and per-source-group corroboration escalates all 18. Both rules halve completion on the two honest benign causes of sec-triage (3 of 6), because the monitoring false positive has only one specific signature there; across the 26 benign explanations of sigma-triage, corroboration escalates 8 of 104 benign episodes. All counts come from one environment, and a 0 of 18 cell bounds attack success only loosely (about 15\%, one-sided 95\%, even if its six causes and three repeats were independent). The rule assumes that the attacker forges one source group; the next section shows what happens when the model reads every source.

\subsection{Readings: When the Model Reads}
\label{sec:rawlog}
Every result so far gives the controller discrete observations. The raw-log studies remove this simplification on sec-triage: each probe returns log text, and a reader turns it into the outcome the ledger consumes. The LLM-free controller plans in the recommended configuration (in v1.3 with source-group corroboration), so the model's only role is reading, and the verifier checks the true outcomes. We compare a rule parser written against the documented format with the served model, under the six log conditions of \S\ref{sec:setup}, and, for every attack, a scripted reader that reads every format correctly and adopts every claim it can see. \Cref{tab:v13} reports the attacks closed as benign under four reader policies.

First, the model is needed for reading. The rule parser reads the documented format without error and cannot read a single drifted line, so under drift every case escalates (0 of 364 observations; 48 of 48 episodes). The models read the drifted format: Qwen2.5-7B parsed 108 of 108 drifted observations and Llama-3.1-8B 174 of 196 in v1.2, and under the recommended controller in v1.3 Qwen2.5-7B completes 41 of 48 drifted episodes, the same as with structured observations, while Llama's misreadings cost 13 verified episodes (28 of 48).

Second, a non-adaptive attack looks contained. The v1.2 injection asserted the authorized scan's reading on four probes. Qwen2.5-7B adopted it in 48 of 105 observations and Llama-3.1-8B once in 71, and no attack was closed as benign, because the adopted readings contradicted the other probes. The scripted reader shows that this was the model's doing, not the ledger's: adopting every claim, it closes all 36 actionable cases as the authorized scan after two probes (2.19 cost units), since the four claimed probes span three source groups, so source-group corroboration is met by forged readings alone.

Third, the adaptive attacks reach that bound on the models. With the claim on every probe in the documented format, Qwen2.5-7B adopted it on \texttt{change\_ticket} (24 of 24) and \texttt{access\_pattern} (24 of 30) but never on \texttt{source\_ips} (0 of 42) or \texttt{threat\_intel} (0 of 11), where the documented record competes with the claim; the reputation group was never forged, and 0 of 36 attacks closed, with 18 escalations. On the drifted format the same claims stand alone, and Qwen2.5-7B adopted all 84: every one of the 36 attacks was closed as the authorized scan, identical to the scripted reader. Llama-3.1-8B adopted 85 of 124 there and closed 19 of 36. Log-line injection, which forges a whole record after the true one, closed nothing on either model, because both read the first \texttt{source\_ips} record (0 of 42 adopted), but Llama adopted the forged \texttt{access\_pattern} and \texttt{change\_ticket} records in 72 of 72 and left all 36 attacks unresolved. The pre-registered endpoints hold: the model alone closes more attacks than under the reader-trust rule by $+0.333$ $[+0.333,+0.333]$ on Qwen2.5-7B and $+0.176$ $[+0.093,+0.259]$ on Llama-3.1-8B (97.5\% intervals over 12 actionable tasks, pooled over the three adaptive attacks).

Fourth, what contains the channel. Reading with the rule parser first is immune wherever the format is documented (0 of 36 under every such attack, 41 of 48 verified) and identical to the model alone under drift. Removing the free-text fields blocks every claim that sits inside a field but not the forged log line, which the scripted reader adopts in full (36 of 36 closed) and Qwen2.5-7B in 48 of 130 observations. The reader-trust rule closed no attack under any condition on either model or on the scripted reader, and costs nothing while the rule parser can read (41 of 48, 3.96 units, as with structured observations). Its price falls on the drifted format, where every honest benign case is escalated (0 of 12 against 6 of 12), and under the drifted adaptive attack every episode spends its full budget: attacks are not closed, they are handed to the analyst.

\subsection{What the Allocation Costs}
\label{sec:cost}
\label{sec:rq-stop}
\label{sec:rq-rank}
\label{sec:fair}
Taking decisions from the model is affordable only if the controller can make them. The decomposition study separates the ranking from the loss of choice with blind configurations, in which the planner receives the same prompt whatever the selector; the stopping study then crosses who selects probes with who may stop (\cref{fig:probe-stop}, Appendix~\ref{app:loop}). The ranking itself helps: with identical planner information, EIG/cost selection beats random selection by $+0.306$ $[+0.14,+0.47]$ on Qwen2.5-7B (pre-registered) and by $+0.26$ to $+0.35$ on every other model that concludes; taking the choice away helps weak models and hurts strong ones. Selecting probes does not make a model conclude: Llama-3.1-8B completed none of its 360 decomposition episodes, and a controller stop lifts it from 0 to 0.917 ($+0.917$ $[+0.79,+1.00]$, pre-registered), whereas Qwen2.5-7B needs both parts (a stop alone lifts it from 0.083 to 0.694, selection adds $+0.222$ $[+0.08,+0.39]$). A controller cannot know in advance which part a model lacks, so it has to supply both.

The posterior stop concludes by elimination, which the verifier rejects: with controller selection and stop, four of the five models fail on the same six episodes, a correct DNS command-and-control verdict without a confirming signature, and strong models that decide when to stop keep probing until that signature appears. The v1.1 study removes this confound by giving the LLM-free controller the confirmation stop of \S\ref{sec:finish} and running every LLM configuration with the guard on both families with the same seeds, tasks, and budgets (\cref{tab:v11fair}). With the verifier's standard, the controller alone completes every case: 72 of 72 sec-triage and 152 of 152 sigma-triage episodes, at 2.54 and 2.20 units. No model adds to it. Qwen2.5-72B deciding when to stop meets the evidence policy in 72 of 72 and 148 of 152 episodes ($+0.000$ $[0.000,0.000]$ and $-0.028$ $[-0.111,0.000]$, the mean over rules of per-rule differences; pre-registered, 97.5\%), at 3.86 and 3.25 units; by the interpretation written before the run, we measured no gain from the LLM in deciding when to stop. Qwen2.5-32B and Llama-3.1-70B behave alike, and every sigma-triage miss of the three large models names the right cause without a confirming signature. Adaptive selection still matters on sec-triage, where a fixed playbook reaches 66 and catalogue order 65, but not on sigma-triage. Qwen2.5-7B falls short under the controller stop on three rules for one reason: it proposes a finish at a posterior just below the guard's threshold and repeats it until the decision budget is spent (11 episodes). The v1.2 recovery rule, which runs the controller's top-ranked probe after two consecutive rejected finishes, turns 152 of 152 stalled sigma-triage episodes of a scripted planner into 152 completions.

The stopping failure is partly a prompting effect, and the baselines might only have lacked a clear prompt. Replaying 60 archived requests with overwhelming evidence or no probe left, Llama-3.1-8B asked for a probe in 60, 60, and 58 of 60 under the original prompt, a prompt with an example finish, and a prompt that states when to finish. The v1.2 study selected, on development seeds and by a rule fixed in advance, the variant that helped the baselines most, and ran every configuration with both prompts (\cref{tab:v12fair}). The clearer prompt doubled Qwen2.5-7B's Memory-only completion (8 to 16 of 48), did not help ReAct-style or either Llama baseline, and made Llama-3.1-8B finish on its own inside \hesp{} in 32 of 48 episodes. The controller's advantage remained: the confirmation stop exceeds Memory-only under the clearer prompt by $+0.667$ $[+0.46,+0.85]$ on Qwen2.5-7B and by $+1.000$ $[+1.000,+1.000]$ on Llama-3.1-8B (pre-registered, 97.5\%).

\subsection{The Evidence Policy and the Tables Behind It}
\label{sec:policy}
\label{sec:mismatch}
\label{sec:rq-help}
The evidence policy is a trade-off between automation and safety, not a detail of scoring. Our verifier accepts a verdict only if one cited observation carries a signature that the cause alone produces; under a ``right cause'' standard the posterior stop would also complete 72 of 72 (\cref{tab:v11fair}). The v1.2 study measures the alternatives with the LLM-free controller (\cref{tab:v12policy}) on \emph{comp-triage}, a third family of three scenarios in which an attack and three legitimate activities use the same tools, with probabilistic outcomes and five of twelve causes without a single-observation signature, using a \emph{joint} verifier and stop rules that require joint evidence against the named causes, or also against \textit{other}. On comp-triage, joint evidence names the right cause in 59 of 72 episodes against 20 for the single-signature rule, which escalates 52. The price is paid in the attacker's currency: joint evidence closed 4 of 18 attacks as the paired legitimate activity, and when the true cause is absent from the candidates it named another actionable cause in 24 of 48 sec-triage episodes; requiring the evidence to beat \textit{other} tenfold did not change this, and thirtyfold only reduced it to 18. Only the single-signature rule named no wrong cause in any setting; lowering its ratio to 3 moves along the same frontier (37 right causes, 1 missed attack). A deployment has to choose a point on this frontier; ours favours escalation.

The controller's knowledge sits in its prediction tables. Counted tables match the generator's: with $k{=}20$ development episodes per cause and variant, \hesp{} with the finish guard lifts Qwen2.5-7B from 0.125 to 1.000 ($+0.875$ $[+0.74,+0.97]$, pre-registered), equal to designer tables on all three Qwen models, and $k{=}5$ already suffices (\cref{tab:v06}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}); the models cannot write the tables themselves, since elicited tables leave completion at 0.069 to 0.375. \Cref{tab:v10b} breaks the link between tables and generator with the LLM-free controller. Imprecise tables make the controller fail by escalating: as random mixing grows, completion falls, but the wrong-cause rate stays at or below 0.009 even when three quarters of every row is noise, and EIG/cost stays ahead of random selection at every level. A cause missing from the development data produces confident wrong verdicts, always of the same class (a missing attack as another actionable cause, a missing benign explanation as another benign one), so no missing cause led to an attack being closed as benign. Errors that random mixing does not produce (v1.1) separate the stop rules: systematic confusion between two causes leaves the posterior stop almost unaffected at weight 0.25 (47 of 48) but blocks the confirmation stop entirely, which spends the full budget and escalates all 48, so the confirmation rule keeps errors low by giving up automation when the table blurs two causes, whereas joint evidence verifies 47 of 48 without a wrong verdict; a cause absent from the candidate set is named as another actionable cause in 24 of 48 episodes under the posterior stop and escalated in all 48 under the confirmation stop. Across thresholds from 0.6 to 0.95 the confirmation stop keeps 1.000 at rising cost (2.54 to 4.14 units), while the posterior stop falls to 0.875 from 0.9 on.

\subsection{Second Task Family}
\label{sec:external}
On sigma-triage, whose candidate causes come from the authors of public detection rules and whose outcomes were annotated by a model outside both planner families, both pre-registered endpoints on Qwen2.5-7B hold (98.33\% intervals over 12 rules; \cref{tab:v10a}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}): the ranking adds $+0.171$ $[+0.094,+0.250]$, and \hesp{} with a controller stop exceeds Memory-only by $+0.296$ $[+0.088,+0.473]$. The ranking effect replicates on all five models ($+0.105$ to $+0.230$), and the separation of probing from stopping replicates: without a controller stop, Llama-3.1-8B finishes in one of 304 episodes, and a stop lifts it to 1.000. These configurations have no finish guard, and Qwen2.5-7B closed 10 of 144 attack episodes as benign inside \hesp{} (none under Memory-only), nine on one rule; in each, the ledger gave the claimed cause 0.002 and none of the cited observations supported it, so the guard would have rejected every one. The sec-triage primary endpoints are also robust to the clustering unit: resampling the 8 causes instead of the 24 tasks leaves every confirmed endpoint's interval above zero, and the effects are similar on static and drifting tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).

\subsection{Fit of Public Security Data to This Evaluation}
\label{sec:realdata}
Would these results hold on real data? We asked whether the three public datasets that come closest could test an investigation policy as defined here, which needs alerts that leave several explanations plausible, probes that differ in cost and informativeness, and objective ground truth (Appendix~\ref{app:public}; the ExCyTIn feasibility checks read its test questions, erratum E-10, whereas GUIDE's test split was never read). In the subsets we examined and with our adaptations, we found no setting that fits this question: most ExCyTIn-Bench~\citep{excytin} training questions name the alert to locate (54.8\%); in GUIDE~\citep{guide}, organization identity carries 1.03 of the grade's 1.50 bits and evidence integration does not rank cold-start true positives better than history lookup (AUROC 0.755 against 0.759); in the 27 OTRF~\citep{otrf} remote-execution recordings we audited, a rule on the alert text separates 44 of 46 alerts. Paired attacks and legitimate administration that use the same tools, under objective labels, are what these subsets lack; the Cyber Defense Benchmark~\citep{cyberdefensebench} shows that such telemetry can be made interactive.
