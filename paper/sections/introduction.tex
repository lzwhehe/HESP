\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Alert triage is a sequential diagnosis problem. An analyst is paged about anomalous activity and must decide what is actually happening. The answer might be an attack, a misconfiguration, authorized work, or a false positive. The analyst works by running queries against logs, configuration, and threat intelligence, and each query costs time. LLM agents are an obvious fit for this loop, and a growing body of work applies them to security operations~\citep{excytin,guide}. Many organizations, however, cannot or will not send raw telemetry to a hosted model. For them, the realistic option is a small open-weight model running on local hardware.

We find that small local models often fail at this task in ways that have little to do with security knowledge. In our pre-registered studies, Qwen2.5-7B choosing its own probes completes only 12.5\% of triage cases with a verified verdict. It repeats queries, exhausts its budget, or never commits to a verdict. Llama-3.1-8B never concludes at all: across 3{,}389 decisions it always asks for one more probe, even when the evidence is overwhelming and no legal probe is left. The models are not missing the facts they need. When the same 7B model is placed inside a controller that decides which probe to run, the same cases are solved. What the small models lack is \emph{investigation discipline}.

This paper presents \hesp{}, a controller that supplies that discipline from outside the model. \hesp{} keeps an explicit ledger of competing explanations for the alert and a table of what each probe is expected to return under each explanation. It picks the next probe by expected information gain per unit cost, and it accepts a verdict only if an observation from the current state supports it. The model still reads the ledger and may propose, finish, or abstain. Every prediction is journaled before the observation it predicts, and an audit checks each episode for consistency. The controller is deliberately simple. Our contribution is not a new selection rule but a careful measurement of \emph{which} parts of such a controller help \emph{which} models, and where it stops helping.

\paragraph{Evaluation discipline.}
LLM-agent results are easy to overstate: prompts get tuned on the test set, comparisons are chosen after the fact, and failures quietly drop out of the denominator. Every study reported here was pre-registered before any of its episodes ran. We froze the protocol, the source hash, and the prediction tables, and we kept every failure, timeout, and malformed output in the denominator. Where an earlier claim turned out to be wrong, we published an erratum instead of rewriting it (\cref{app:errata}). Tables are generated by scripts from the raw outcome files.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A controller for local triage agents} (\cref{sec:design}): a Bayesian hypothesis ledger, cost-aware information-gain probe selection, a state-guarded finish, an independent verifier, and a write-ahead journal with an audit. The controller can also stop the investigation itself, under the same evidence rule.
  \item \textbf{Non-oracle prediction tables} (\cref{sec:rq2}): probe-outcome tables estimated only from LLM-free development episodes recover all of the benefit of oracle tables. Tables elicited from the LLM itself do not.
  \item \textbf{A decomposition of where the benefit comes from} (\cref{sec:rq3}): a randomized control that holds the planner's information fixed attributes $+0.26$ to $+0.35$ of completion to the information-gain ranking on every concluding model. Handing probe choice to the controller helps a 7B model but hurts 32B and 72B models.
  \item \textbf{Separating what to probe from when to stop} (\cref{sec:rq4}): choosing probes does not make a model conclude. A controller-side stopping rule rescues a model that never concludes. For that model, however, the stopping rule accounts for the entire gain, and probe selection adds nothing measurable. Different small models lack different parts of the procedure.
  \item \textbf{A measurement of why public SOC datasets cannot test investigation policies} (\cref{sec:realdata}), based on three datasets, and a real-log case study of a masquerading scheduled task.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.} All confirmatory results come from a controlled, hand-built environment in which the verifier knows the ground truth. We make no claim about performance on real SOC data. \Cref{sec:realdata} explains why we could not make one with public data, and \cref{sec:discussion} lists the limitations.
