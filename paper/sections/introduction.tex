\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Alert triage is a sequential diagnosis problem. An analyst paged about anomalous activity must decide what is actually happening: an attack, a misconfiguration, authorized work, or a false positive. The analyst works by querying logs, configuration, and threat intelligence, and every query costs time. LLM agents fit this loop naturally, and recent work applies them to security investigation~\citep{excytin,guide}. Many organizations, however, cannot or will not send raw telemetry to a hosted model. For them, the realistic option is a small open-weight model on local hardware.

Small local models fail at this loop in procedural ways. In our pre-registered studies, Qwen2.5-7B choosing its own probes ends only 12.5\% of triage cases with a verified verdict. It repeats queries and exhausts its budget. Llama-3.1-8B never concludes at all. Across 3{,}389 decisions it always asks for one more probe, even when the evidence is overwhelming and no legal probe is left. Nor can the models supply the missing structure themselves: when Qwen2.5-7B writes its own probe-outcome likelihoods, completion stays at 0.069.

\hesp{} moves the investigation procedure out of the model. The controller keeps an explicit ledger of competing explanations and a table of what each probe is expected to return under each explanation. It picks the next probe by expected information gain per unit cost and accepts a verdict only if an observation from the current state supports it. It can also end the investigation itself. The model reads the ledger and may propose probes, finish, or abstain. Every prediction is journaled before its observation, and an audit checks each episode. Each component is standard. Our contribution is a measurement of which part of such a controller helps which model, and of where the model is still needed.

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The procedure transfers without an oracle} (\cref{sec:rq-help,sec:rq-tables}). Probe-outcome tables counted from LLM-free development episodes give the same completion as tables taken from the environment's generator. For Qwen2.5-7B the gain over the model alone is $+0.875$ $[+0.74,+0.97]$.
  \item \textbf{The ranking helps every model; taking the choice away helps only weak ones} (\cref{sec:rq-rank}). With the planner's information held fixed, information-gain ranking beats random probe selection by $+0.26$ to $+0.35$ on all four models that conclude. Replacing the model's own choices with random ones helps the 7B model and hurts the 32B and 72B models.
  \item \textbf{What to probe and when to stop are separate failures} (\cref{sec:rq-stop}). A controller-side stopping rule rescues Llama-3.1-8B from 0 to 0.917. After that, controller selection adds no detectable completion for it, whereas Qwen2.5-7B needs both.
  \item \textbf{The controller alone comes close} (\cref{sec:rq-stop}). A post hoc run with no LLM completes 0.917 of cases at the lowest probe cost of any configuration. Strong models deciding when to stop reach 1.000, because they wait for evidence that confirms the cause instead of concluding by elimination. The remaining roles for a local model are this judgment about when evidence is sufficient and the translation of raw telemetry into the discrete observations the controller consumes. Our environment supplies those observations directly.
  \item \textbf{Public SOC datasets cannot yet test investigation policies} (\cref{sec:realdata}). In ExCyTIn-Bench the question names the answer, in GUIDE labels follow organizational policy, and in OTRF the attack tooling gives itself away. A real-log case study shows the kind of case they lack.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Evaluation discipline.}
Every study was pre-registered: the protocol, the controller source hash, and the prediction tables were frozen before any of its episodes ran. Failures, timeouts, and malformed outputs stay in the denominator. Errors in our own earlier reports appear as errata (\cref{app:errata}), and scripts generate every table from the raw outcome files. The confirmatory evidence comes from one controlled environment whose verifier knows the ground truth; \cref{sec:realdata} explains why public data could not replace it.
