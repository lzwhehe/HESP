\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Security operations centers receive far more alerts than analysts can investigate, and most of those alerts turn out to be benign~\citep{nodoze}. LLM agents promise relief: they query logs, read the results, and decide what caused an alert~\citep{excytin,clouseau}. Organizations that may not send telemetry to a hosted model can run such an agent on a small open-weight model inside their own network. That choice keeps the data in-house, but it puts every step of the investigation in the hands of a weaker model, and it hands part of the model's input to the attacker.

The attacker's leverage is easy to see. Whoever triggers an alert also writes some of what the agent reads: a crafted user agent, a command line, or a ticket comment is copied into the logs the agent queries. The attacker needs only one outcome, a benign verdict, because a benign verdict closes the case without any response. Log-borne prompt injection against LLM log analysis has been demonstrated~\citep{loginject}, and in our experiments every model that decides a verdict on its own follows an instruction planted in a log field to close the alert.

Existing defenses do not fit this setting well. Prompting and fine-tuning defenses~\citep{struq,secalign} make a model less likely to follow injected instructions but give no guarantee, and small local models are exactly the models that follow them. Isolation and information-flow designs~\citep{camel,fides,isolategpt,progent} keep untrusted text away from the component that plans tool calls; a triage agent, however, has no dangerous tool to guard, since all its probes are read-only. Its harmful output is the verdict itself, and the verdict is computed from the very data the attacker writes into. Finally, a small model is a poor investigator even without an attacker: in our measurements, Qwen2.5-7B choosing its own queries resolves 8 to 13\% of cases, and Llama-3.1-8B never commits to a verdict.

Our key idea is to let the model read but not decide. We present \hesp{}, a controller that takes every decision that can close a case away from the model. \hesp{} keeps a Bayesian ledger of candidate causes, chooses read-only probes by expected information gain per cost, and accepts a verdict only if an observation in the ledger supports it; the model proposes next steps and, when logs are raw text, parses them.

Realizing this idea requires addressing three challenges. First, the controller must investigate at least as well as the model, or operators will prefer the model. \hesp{} separates the two decisions that small models fail at, choosing what to probe and deciding when to stop, and makes both from counted probe-outcome tables. Second, once the controller decides, the attacker forges evidence instead of text, and the controller's own preference for the most informative probe makes a single forged source decisive. \hesp{} requires a benign verdict to be corroborated by two distinct source groups. Third, the model is still needed to read logs whose format a fixed parser cannot handle, and a model that reads can be talked into the attacker's reading of every log at once. \hesp{} records which parser produced each observation and never lets a model-parsed observation support a benign verdict.

We evaluate \hesp{} on three simulated triage settings, one of them derived from public Sigma detection rules, with five open-weight models from 7B to 72B and three classes of attack, including adaptive attacks written against \hesp{} itself. No injected instruction, forged source, or attack on the model's reading closes a case under \hesp{}, whereas without its rules up to every attack is closed as benign. Taking the decisions from the model costs no resolved cases: the controller alone resolves every case in two of the three settings, and neither a 72B planner nor a prompt tuned for the baselines improves on it.

In summary, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \hesp{}, a controller that lets a local LLM read but not decide in alert triage, with three rules that close the attacker's paths to the verdict: a finish guard, source-diverse corroboration, and reader trust (\S\ref{sec:approach}).
  \item An analysis of how an attacker reaches the verdict of a local triage agent, including an attack on model-based log parsing that defeats source corroboration by writing into every log (\S\ref{sec:threat}, \S\ref{sec:rawlog}).
  \item An evaluation on three settings with five models and adaptive attacks, which also shows that small models fail at choosing probes and at stopping for different reasons (\S\ref{sec:results}).\footnote{Code, prediction tables, outcome files, and episode journals are available at \repourl.}
\end{itemize}
