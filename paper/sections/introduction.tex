\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Security operations face a growing tension between the number of alerts and the analyst time available to explain them: most alerts turn out to be benign~\citep{nodoze}, yet each must be traced to a cause before it can be closed. To relieve this tension, triage has been automated so as to decide, for each alert, whether an attack, a misconfiguration, authorized activity, or a false positive caused it. Two typical examples of such automation are provenance-based ranking~\citep{nodoze,kairos} and LLM investigation agents~\citep{excytin,clouseau}. Provenance-based systems build causal graphs of host activity and rank or summarize them, so that analysts read less; however, they decide what an analyst should look at, not which query to run next, and they leave the verdict to the analyst. LLM investigation agents choose queries and read their results in the ReAct pattern~\citep{react}, but the strongest of them rely on hosted models, to which many organizations cannot or will not send raw telemetry.

As an alternative that keeps telemetry on the premises, triage agents built on small open-weight models have become practical, which run on a single local GPU, choose their own queries, and return a verdict without an analyst in the loop. At a high level, such an agent receives an alert and a catalogue of read-only queries, such as authentication logs, request patterns, source reputation, configuration, change tickets, and threat intelligence; at each step, the model reads the results so far and either runs another query or states a cause together with the evidence it cites. Once the agent concludes, a benign verdict closes the case without any response, and any other verdict escalates it to an analyst.

However, given how much of this loop rests on the model, little is known about how reliably small local models carry out the investigation procedure, and to what extent an attacker can steer the verdict. It is also unclear which of the model's decisions an attacker can reach, whether a component outside the model can take those decisions over, and what such a component costs in accuracy. These questions matter because part of what a triage agent reads is written by the attacker: request payloads, user agents, command lines, and ticket text all pass through the logs, and indirect prompt injection through retrieved content is well established~\citep{greshake2023,agentdojo}. In this paper, we report a controlled security study of local LLM triage agents, which answers these questions with a controller that can take each decision from the model, three simulated triage settings with attackers, eight studies frozen before their first episode, and a rule that closes each path we found.

Our study is made possible through three components. First of all, a controller, \hesp{}, has been built to hold the procedure outside the model: it keeps a Bayesian ledger of candidate causes, ranks read-only probes by expected information gain per cost, accepts a verdict only if a current observation in the ledger supports it, and can end the investigation itself. Each of these decisions can be handed back to the model, so that one agent can be studied under every allocation of control. In addition, to measure where the attacker gets in, a triage testbed has been designed in which every episode runs against its own loopback sandbox and the attacker writes instructions into log fields, forges a data source, or targets the model while it parses raw logs. The public datasets we audited lack paired attacks and look-alike administration under objective labels (\S\ref{sec:realdata}), so the testbed provides three simulated settings: one whose causes we wrote, one derived mechanically from public Sigma detection rules, and one in which an attack and legitimate activity use the same tools. Given dozens of configurations on five models, it is easy to find an effect by looking for one. To conquer this challenge, each study fixed its hypotheses, primary endpoints, and source hash before its first episode, and an audit replayed the journal of each of the 23{,}582 LLM episodes.

Leveraging these components, our study has distilled a set of findings, which are summarized below.

First of all, small local models fail at the investigation procedure itself, and in two different ways. Qwen2.5-7B choosing its own probes ends only 8 to 13\% of cases with a verified verdict while spending nearly its whole budget, and under our prompt and serving configuration, Llama-3.1-8B asks for another probe in all 3{,}389 of its decisions. Larger models conclude, but cite invalid evidence in 22 to 31\% of their citations. Choosing what to probe and deciding when to stop are separate failures, so a controller has to take over both (\S\ref{sec:rq-stop}).

Furthermore, when the model decides, an instruction planted in a log field closes the case. Under four injection texts, every model that decides and reaches its own verdict follows an instruction to dismiss the alert. A finish guard that accepts only verdicts backed by the ledger blocks it on every model, since the text never enters the ledger, and the injection is reduced to cases left unresolved (\S\ref{sec:injection}).

Besides, once the controller decides, the attacker must forge evidence instead of text, and one forged source suffices. A single forged scanner answer, or a poisoned reputation feed read by two probes, closes 18 of 18 actionable cases as benign on every model, because the controller acts on its most informative observation first. Requiring support from two distinct source groups for a benign verdict escalates all of them, at a cost of 3 of 6 honest benign cases in one setting and 8 of 104 in another (\S\ref{sec:evidence}).

What is even more worrying is that the one role left to the model reopens the attack. When the log format changes, a rule parser reads nothing and every case escalates, so the model has to read the logs. An attacker who writes a consistent benign story into the free-text field of every log then reaches every source at once: a reader that adopts every claim closes all 36 actionable cases as an authorized scan, Qwen2.5-7B does the same on a changed log format, and source-group corroboration does not help. A reader-trust rule, under which the model's readings may confirm an attack but never a benign verdict, lets no attack close a case on either model (\S\ref{sec:rawlog}).

Also, taking the decisions from the model costs no accuracy in our settings. With the verifier's evidence standard, the controller alone resolves every case in two of the three settings; a pre-registered comparison finds no gain from letting Qwen2.5-72B decide when to stop, and baselines given a prompt selected to help them still fall short by $+0.667$ and $+1.000$. Where causes share tools, the evidence standard trades automation for safety: joint evidence resolves more cases, but closes 4 of 18 attacks as the legitimate activity they resemble (\S\ref{sec:cost}).

Our contributions can be outlined as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We conduct a controlled security study of local LLM alert-triage agents, which locates three paths by which an attacker reaches the verdict, one for each allocation of control.
  \item We design and implement \hesp{}\footnote{Code, frozen protocols, prediction tables, outcome files, and the episode journals of every study except the first (erratum E-2) are available at \repourl.}, a controller that can take each triage decision from the model, together with three rules that close the paths we found: a finish guard, source-group corroboration, and reader trust.
  \item We build a triage testbed with three simulated settings and five kinds of attacker, and run eight studies under frozen protocols with audited journals, scripted upper bounds for every reader attack, and a record of our own errors.
  \item We distill a set of findings with supporting data: how small models fail at the procedure, where each attack succeeds and fails, and what each rule costs.
\end{itemize}
