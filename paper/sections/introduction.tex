\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Alert triage is a sequential diagnosis problem. An analyst paged about anomalous activity must decide what is actually happening: an attack, a misconfiguration, authorized work, or a false positive. The analyst works by querying logs, configuration, and threat intelligence, and every query costs time. LLM agents fit this loop naturally, and recent work applies them to security investigation~\citep{excytin,guide}. Many organizations, however, cannot or will not send raw telemetry to a hosted model. For them, the realistic option is a small open-weight model on local hardware.

Small local models fail at this loop in procedural ways, and current agent designs leave the procedure to the model. \Cref{fig:motivating} shows one alert handled by Llama-3.1-8B: the model finds the decisive evidence and then keeps probing until its budget is gone, never stating a verdict. In our pre-registered studies, Qwen2.5-7B choosing its own probes ends only 8 to 13\% of triage cases with a verified verdict: it keeps probing until its budget runs out and issues a verdict in fewer than one case in ten. Llama-3.1-8B never concludes. Across 3{,}389 decisions it always asks for one more probe, even when the evidence is overwhelming and no legal probe is left. The models also cannot supply the structure themselves: when Qwen2.5-7B writes its own probe-outcome likelihoods, completion stays at 0.069. Evaluations that report only end-task success cannot tell these failures apart, and public SOC datasets turn out to be unable to test investigation at all (\cref{sec:realdata}).

Our key insight is that an investigation consists of two decisions that need not belong to the same party: \emph{what to probe next} and \emph{when the evidence suffices}. A controller outside the model can own either or both, using an explicit belief over competing explanations. Our thesis is that \emph{an investigation procedure held outside the model makes small local LLMs reliable alert-triage agents, and that separating what to probe from when to stop reveals which part of the procedure each model lacks.} \hesp{} implements this procedure as a controller (\cref{fig:pipeline}). It keeps a ledger of competing explanations and a table of what each probe is expected to return under each explanation. It selects probes by expected information gain per unit cost, accepts only verdicts backed by current evidence, and can end the investigation itself. The model reads the ledger and may propose probes, finish, or abstain. Every prediction is journaled before its observation, and every episode is audited.

We evaluate \hesp{} in four pre-registered studies with five open-weight models from two families (7B to 72B) and 7{,}272 audited episodes. Probe-outcome tables counted from LLM-free development episodes lift Qwen2.5-7B from 0.125 to 1.000 verified completion, matching tables taken from the environment's generator. With the planner's information held fixed, the information-gain ranking adds $+0.26$ to $+0.35$ on every model that concludes. A controller-side stop lifts Llama-3.1-8B from 0 to 0.917, after which controller probe selection adds no detectable completion for it; Qwen2.5-7B needs both parts. The controller alone, with no LLM, completes 0.917 of cases, and the best LLM configurations reach 1.000 because strong models wait for confirming evidence before they finish.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controller for local triage agents that owns the hypothesis ledger, probe selection, verdict acceptance, and stopping, with a write-ahead journal and a per-episode audit (\cref{sec:overview,sec:design,sec:impl}).
  \item A pre-registered evaluation methodology for LLM investigation agents: frozen protocols and source hashes, blind controls that hold the planner's prompt fixed across selectors, a factorial design that separates probe selection from stopping, and published errata (\cref{sec:method}, \cref{app:prereg,app:errata}).
  \item Evidence that the controller needs no oracle (RQ1), and a decomposition of its gain into the loss of the model's choice and the information-gain ranking, which helps every model while taking the choice away helps only weak ones (RQ2; \cref{sec:rq-help,sec:rq-rank}).
  \item Evidence that what to probe and when to stop are separate failures, and an LLM-free reference that locates the model's remaining contribution in judging when evidence suffices (RQ3; \cref{sec:rq-stop}).
  \item A measurement of three public security datasets showing why none can test investigation policies, and a real-log case study of the kind of case they lack (\cref{sec:realdata}).
\end{enumerate}

All confirmatory evidence comes from one controlled environment whose verifier knows the ground truth. Failures, timeouts, and malformed outputs stay in every denominator, and scripts generate every table from the raw outcome files.
