\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When an alert fires, an analyst has to decide what caused it: an attack in progress, a misconfiguration, authorized activity, or a false positive. The analyst narrows the candidate explanations by querying authentication logs, request patterns, configuration, change tickets, and threat intelligence, and stops once some observation supports one explanation and the others have been ruled out. The verdict decides whether the incident is escalated, and every query costs time.

Doing this by hand does not scale. Alert volumes exceed what analysts can investigate, and most alerts turn out to be benign~\citep{nodoze}. LLM agents that choose queries and read their results fit this loop naturally. Many organizations, however, cannot or will not send raw telemetry to a hosted model. For them the realistic option is an open-weight model small enough to run on a single local GPU, and recent work on LLM-driven attack investigation already reports that such models fall behind proprietary ones on complex cases~\citep{clouseau}.

Efforts to automate triage and investigation follow two broad approaches. The first builds provenance graphs of host activity and ranks or summarizes them with fixed rules or learned models~\citep{nodoze,kairos}. These systems reduce what an analyst has to read, but they do not decide which query to run next. The second applies LLM agents that choose queries and interpret the results~\citep{excytin,clouseau}, in the ReAct pattern where the model decides every step~\citep{react}. These agents leave the investigation procedure to the model, and with small local models the procedure is exactly what fails. In our studies, Qwen2.5-7B choosing its own probes ends only 8 to 13\% of triage cases with a verified verdict: it keeps probing until its budget runs out. Under our prompt and serving configuration, Llama-3.1-8B does not conclude: across 3{,}389 decisions it asks for one more probe every time, even when the evidence is overwhelming and no legal probe is left. Larger models do conclude, but without guidance they cite invalid evidence in 22 to 31\% of their citations and call 13 of 51 and 20 of 54 of the actionable incidents they rule on benign.

This paper asks which of these decisions should be taken from the model, and what security boundary results. An investigation consists of two decisions, \emph{what to probe next} and \emph{when the evidence suffices}, and a controller can own either or both. We build \hesp{}, a controller that keeps a ledger of candidate causes updated by Bayes' rule from counted probe-outcome tables, ranks read-only probes by expected information gain per cost, accepts only verdicts backed by a current supporting observation, and can end the investigation itself; the model remains a planner that may propose probes, finish, or abstain. Cost-aware test selection and hypothesis identification are classical~\citep{dekleer1987,golovin2010}, and \hesp{} uses the textbook criterion unchanged. What is new is the measured allocation of control between such a criterion and an LLM, and the security consequences of each allocation.

We evaluate this as a controlled mechanism study on three synthetic task families with predefined causes, two of them nearly deterministic and one with probabilistic, overlapping outcomes: seven studies with internally frozen, prospective protocols, five open-weight models (7B to 72B), and 21{,}278 audited LLM episodes. Three results organize the paper. First, choosing what to probe and deciding when to stop are separate failures: with the planner's information held fixed, the information-gain ranking adds $+0.26$ to $+0.35$ on every model that concludes, while a controller-side stop is what rescues Llama-3.1-8B (from 0 to 0.917) and Qwen2.5-7B needs both. Second, once the controller applies the verifier's evidence standard, it completes every case in sec-triage and sigma-triage without any LLM, and a pre-registered comparison finds no gain from letting Qwen2.5-72B decide when to stop; the earlier advantage of strong models that ``wait for confirmation'' was the advantage of a stricter evidence rule. That rule is a point on a frontier: accepting joint evidence resolves more cases in which causes share tools, but closes some attacks as the legitimate activity they resemble and names a wrong cause whenever the true one is not among the candidates. With an equally clear prompt, the baselines still fall far short. The one role left for the model is reading raw logs, where small models handle a changed format that defeats a rule parser but can be talked into an attacker's reading. Third, the security boundary is sharp. Under the four injection texts we tested, every model that decides and reaches its own verdict follows an instruction to dismiss the alert, and the finish guard blocks it on every model; a single forged source defeats the controller, because it acts on the most informative evidence first, and requiring corroboration from two source groups for benign verdicts turns those cases into escalations, at a cost on honest benign cases that depends on how many signatures they have.

In summary, the contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{A decomposition of control.} We show, with blind controls, that probe selection and stopping are separate failures of small local LLMs that differ between models of similar size, and that with an explicit confirmation rule a controller without an LLM completes every case in our two deterministic families, while no model we tested adds to it; accepting joint evidence instead trades wrong verdicts for automation (\S\ref{sec:rq-stop}, \S\ref{sec:fair}).
  \item \emph{The security boundary of each allocation.} We attribute the security behaviour to components: the finish guard blocks injected dismissals, a forged source defeats early stopping, the confirmation rule turns an absent cause into escalations rather than wrong verdicts, and source-group corroboration restores safety at a measured cost (\S\ref{sec:adversarial}, \S\ref{sec:mismatch}).
  \item \emph{A controlled, reproducible evaluation.} \hesp{}\footnote{Code, frozen protocols, prediction tables, outcome files, and the episode journals of every study except the first (erratum E-2) are available at \repourl.}, internally frozen protocols with source hashes, LLM-free references at the same evidence standard, table-error tests, two further task families, raw-log parsing, an audit of three public datasets, and a record of our own errors (\S\ref{sec:setup}, Appendix~\ref{app:errata}).
\end{itemize}
