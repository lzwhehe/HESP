\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When an alert fires, an analyst has to decide what caused it: an attack in progress, a misconfiguration, authorized activity, or a false positive. The analyst narrows the candidate explanations by querying authentication logs, request patterns, configuration, change tickets, and threat intelligence, and stops once some observation supports one explanation and the others have been ruled out. The verdict decides whether the incident is escalated, and every query costs time.

Doing this by hand does not scale. Alert volumes exceed what analysts can investigate, and most alerts turn out to be benign~\citep{nodoze}. LLM agents that choose queries and read their results fit this loop naturally. Many organizations, however, cannot or will not send raw telemetry to a hosted model. For them the realistic option is an open-weight model small enough to run on a single local GPU, and recent work on LLM-driven attack investigation already reports that such models fall behind proprietary ones on complex cases~\citep{clouseau}. Two facts make this a security problem rather than only a capability problem. First, part of the evidence the agent reads is written by the attacker: request payloads, user agents, process command lines, and ticket text all pass through the logs, and indirect prompt injection through retrieved content is well established~\citep{greshake2023,agentdojo}. Second, the attacker's goal in triage is specific and cheap: not to take over the agent, but to have the alert closed as benign, or left unresolved.

Existing approaches leave the whole procedure to the model. Provenance-based systems rank or summarize host activity~\citep{nodoze,kairos} but do not decide which query to run next; LLM investigation agents~\citep{excytin,clouseau} follow the ReAct pattern~\citep{react}, in which the model chooses every probe, reads every result, and decides when to stop. With small local models, the procedure is exactly what fails. In our studies, Qwen2.5-7B choosing its own probes ends only 8 to 13\% of triage cases with a verified verdict; under our prompt and serving configuration, Llama-3.1-8B never concludes in 3{,}389 decisions; larger models conclude but cite invalid evidence in 22 to 31\% of their citations. And when we plant an instruction to dismiss the alert in a log field, every model that decides and reaches its own verdict follows it.

This paper asks \emph{where the attacker gets in}, as a function of which decisions the model is allowed to make. An investigation consists of three activities: choosing what to probe, reading each result, and deciding when the evidence suffices. We build \hesp{}, a controller that keeps a ledger of candidate causes updated by Bayes' rule from counted probe-outcome tables, ranks read-only probes by expected information gain per cost, accepts only verdicts backed by a current supporting observation, and can end the investigation itself; the model remains a planner that may propose probes, finish, or abstain, and, when the logs are raw text, a reader that turns them into the ledger's vocabulary. Cost-aware test selection and hypothesis identification are classical~\citep{dekleer1987,golovin2010}, and \hesp{} uses the textbook criterion unchanged. What is new is the measured attack surface of each allocation of control between such a criterion and a local LLM, together with the rule that closes each channel and what that rule costs.

We evaluate this as a controlled mechanism study on three synthetic task families with predefined causes: eight studies with internally frozen, prospective protocols, five open-weight models (7B to 72B), and 23{,}582 audited LLM episodes. Three channels organize the results, one per allocation. \emph{When the model decides}, injected instructions succeed on every model; a finish guard that accepts only ledger-backed verdicts blocks them on every model, and the injection is reduced to a cost in unresolved cases. \emph{When the controller decides}, the attacker has to forge evidence rather than text, and one forged source suffices, because the controller acts on its most informative observation first; requiring corroboration from two distinct source groups for benign verdicts turns those cases into escalations, at a cost on honest benign cases that depends on how many signatures they have. \emph{When the model reads}, which it must once a format change defeats the rule parser, the attacker writes into every source at once: a consistent benign story in every log's free-text field defeats source-group corroboration under a scripted reader that adopts every claim, and closes attacks as the authorized scan on Qwen2.5-7B (\S\ref{sec:rawlog}). A reader-trust rule, under which model-parsed observations may confirm an attack but never a benign verdict, closes the channel; its price is paid by honest benign cases whenever the rule parser cannot read. Finally, the allocation is affordable: choosing what to probe and deciding when to stop are separate failures of small models, and with the verifier's evidence standard the controller completes every case in sec-triage and sigma-triage without any LLM. A pre-registered comparison finds no gain from letting Qwen2.5-72B decide when to stop, and baselines given an equally clear prompt still fall far short. The standard itself is a trade-off: joint evidence resolves more cases in which causes share tools but closes some attacks as the legitimate activity they resemble.

In summary, the contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The attack surface of control allocation.} We attribute the security behaviour of a local-LLM triage agent to who holds each decision: injected instructions succeed when the model decides and the finish guard blocks them; a forged source defeats a controller that decides, and source-group corroboration restores safety; a model that reads reopens the channel through every source at once, and a reader-trust rule closes it (\S\ref{sec:injection}--\S\ref{sec:rawlog}).
  \item \emph{The cost of the allocation.} With blind controls we show that probe selection and stopping are separate failures that differ between models of similar size; that with an explicit confirmation rule a controller without an LLM completes every case in our two deterministic families, while no model we tested adds to it, even with a prompt selected to help the baselines; and that accepting joint evidence trades wrong verdicts for automation (\S\ref{sec:cost}, \S\ref{sec:policy}).
  \item \emph{A controlled, reproducible evaluation.} \hesp{}\footnote{Code, frozen protocols, prediction tables, outcome files, and the episode journals of every study except the first (erratum E-2) are available at \repourl.}, internally frozen protocols with source hashes, LLM-free references at the same evidence standard, a scripted upper bound for every reader attack, table-error tests, two further task families, an audit of three public datasets, and a record of our own errors (\S\ref{sec:setup}, Appendix~\ref{app:errata}).
\end{itemize}
