\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked where an attacker gets into alert triage by a small local LLM, and found that the answer follows the allocation of control. \hesp{} holds the procedure outside the model: a ledger of candidate causes, information-gain probe selection, an evidence rule, and a controller-side stop. When the model decides, injected instructions succeed on every model and the finish guard blocks them; when the controller decides, one forged source is decisive and corroboration across source groups restores safety; when the model reads raw logs, the attacker writes into every source at once, corroboration no longer helps, and a reader-trust rule that lets model-parsed readings confirm an attack but never a benign verdict closes the channel at a cost paid by honest benign cases. The allocation costs nothing we could measure in three synthetic families: with the verifier's evidence standard the controller alone completes every case in the two deterministic families, no model adds to it, and clearer prompts do not rescue the baselines; the standard itself trades automation for safety where causes share tools. Carrying these results over to real telemetry requires paired, objectively labeled data.
