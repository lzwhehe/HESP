\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLM agents for investigation and triage} Very few works study how LLM agents investigate security alerts, and none that we know of measures what an attacker who writes into the evidence can achieve. Clouseau~\citep{clouseau} reconstructs attack narratives from incident logs with a hierarchy of LLM agents that choose their own queries, and reports that open-weight models trail a proprietary one on complex scenarios; its authors note that narrative reconstruction exceeds what triage, which seeks a quick accept-or-escalate decision, requires. ExCyTIn-Bench~\citep{excytin}, GUIDE~\citep{guide}, and the Cyber Defense Benchmark~\citep{cyberdefensebench} provide data for investigation and threat hunting, whose fit to this question we discuss in \S\ref{sec:realdata}. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to locate the paths by which an attacker reaches a triage agent's verdict as a function of which decisions the model holds; for each path, we have measured the attack on five models, stated the rule that closes it, and measured what the rule costs.

\paragraph{Attacks on and defenses for tool-using agents} Indirect prompt injection, in which instructions hidden in retrieved content hijack an LLM-integrated application, was demonstrated by Greshake et al.~\citep{greshake2023}, and AgentDojo~\citep{agentdojo} benchmarks such attacks and defenses on tool-using agents; log injection through unescaped user-controlled fields is an older instance of the same problem~\citep{owasp_loginjection}. Work on securing agents constrains what an agent may do: IsolateGPT~\citep{isolategpt} isolates the execution of LLM apps, and Progent~\citep{progent} enforces privilege policies on tool calls. In general tool-using agents such as ReAct~\citep{react}, Reflexion~\citep{reflexion}, and Toolformer~\citep{toolformer}, the model decides what to do next and when to finish. \hesp{} shares the premise that an agent should be governed outside the model, but a triage agent only reads, so it governs the verdict (which probe runs, which verdict is accepted, when to stop, and which readings may support a benign verdict) rather than access rights.

\paragraph{Provenance-based triage} Provenance-based systems build causal graphs of host activity and make them tractable with rules or learned models: NoDoze~\citep{nodoze} ranks alerts by the anomaly of their surrounding provenance to combat alert fatigue, and Kairos~\citep{kairos} detects and reconstructs intrusions from whole-system provenance. These systems decide what an analyst should look at, not which query to run next; \hesp{} targets that next step and the verdict that follows it.

\paragraph{Sequential diagnosis} Choosing tests by expected information gain goes back to Lindley~\citep{lindley1956} and Bayesian experimental design~\citep{chaloner1995}. It underlies classic model-based diagnosis~\citep{dekleer1987} and has near-optimality guarantees for greedy active learning and noisy hypothesis identification~\citep{dasgupta2004,golovin2010}. We use the textbook criterion unchanged and ask how it interacts with LLM planners; language models can be calibrated on some self-assessment tasks~\citep{kadavath2022}, but our elicited tables show that this does not extend to probe-outcome likelihoods. Pre-registration separates confirmatory from exploratory analysis~\citep{nosek2018}, and pitfalls in ML-based security evaluation are well documented~\citep{arp2022dos}; we follow both.
